# How is freezing risk accounted for in potential distribution models?

A winter minimum can kill an overwintering plant, while a much milder frost after budburst can damage its leaves or prevent it from producing viable fruit. Both processes can restrict where a species persists, but they call for different climate data. This review examines how plant distribution models, particularly those for woody species, represent exposure to cold, connect exposure to injury, and test whether that injury helps explain a range boundary.

## Winter climate as a proxy for freezing risk

A common approach is to relate observed presences and absences to a summary of winter climate. In their survey of plant distribution modelling, Gardner et al. (2019, Table 1) ranked the minimum temperature of the coldest period (BIO6) sixth among the most frequently used climatic predictors. Van der Meersch et al. (2025) give a concrete example. They chose minimum temperature of the coldest month to represent winter frost tolerance in correlative models of European trees. This choice permits comparisons across species for which injury measurements are scarce. Its limitation follows from the averaging period. Two sites can have the same coldest-month value even if one experiences a single severe freeze and the other a succession of moderately cold nights. A fitted association with BIO6 alone cannot distinguish those exposures or establish which one limits survival.

## From cold events to species-specific injury

Observations at a moving range edge can help identify which aspect of cold to model. Along Florida’s east coast, Cavanaugh et al. (2014) compared 28 years of satellite-derived mangrove cover with weather-station records. Mangrove expansion was associated with a declining frequency of days below −4 °C. Changes in mean annual temperature showed no corresponding association. The response measured here was change in forest area, so the result supports counting severe freezes as a candidate predictor of habitat suitability. Applying the Florida threshold to another species or coastline would require evidence that the same exposure causes comparable damage there.

Physiological measurements offer an independent basis for choosing a threshold. Baranger et al. (2024) calculated a frost safety margin for European tree species by comparing local minimum temperature with the species’ LT₅₀, a temperature associated with lysis of 50% of cells in freezing tests. This expresses how close each site comes to a biologically meaningful limit. Among 30 species with occurrence data that covered the cold range margin, the selected model included the frost safety margin for 26. Occurrence probability generally fell sharply near a margin of zero. The sampling condition matters. If surveys end before they reach colder, unoccupied sites, even a well-measured injury threshold is difficult to test against the distribution.

## Seasonal vulnerability and model evaluation

A single tolerance value is less informative when the vulnerable tissue changes through the year. Ponsa et al. (2026) used the process-based model PHENOFIT to predict suitable growing areas for sweet chestnut varieties in the French Cévennes. They specified −30 °C as a lethal frost threshold and −2 °C as the temperature causing 50% leaf damage. The model also tracked flowering and fruit development. For the late variety Pellegrine, it projected a higher proportion of flowers and fruit escaping frost damage under future conditions. The distinction has a practical consequence. Counting every spring frost gives the same weight to a cold night before flowering and one when flowers are exposed. Predicting flowering dates lets the model distinguish their effects on reproduction.

Testing the resulting maps requires observations beyond those used to fit them. Van der Meersch et al. (2025) compared predictions with fossil pollen records. Correlative models fitted present distributions closely, while process-explicit models performed better under the more dissimilar climates of the early Holocene. The models differed in several processes, so the comparison leaves open how much of that advantage came from frost representation alone.

For a comparison of statistically downscaled climate databases, these findings suggest a specific test. Over a common historical period, compare products against independent station records for the coldest-month summary, the frequency of damaging minima, and, where phenological observations exist, the frequency of freezes after budburst. Then hold the occurrence data and modelling method fixed and examine where changing the climate product moves the predicted cold range edge. This would identify whether disagreement between databases concerns ordinary winter conditions, rare cold nights, or the timing of those nights relative to vulnerable growth.
